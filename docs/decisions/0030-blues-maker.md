# 0030: The blues maker, with sounds from ElevenLabs

**Status:** Accepted, 2026-11-22. Adds `/jam/` to the page list (PLAN.md section 3). Builds on the 12-bar strum (ADR 0018). The instrument and drum sounds (generated with ElevenLabs and retuned) are superseded by ADR 0031: real samples from MuseScore General. The room loops are still generated.

## Context

Joe asked for an interactive blues song maker: pick the instruments, the beat and the rest, with the sounds generated by ElevenLabs.

The site has no server (ADR 0027), the ElevenLabs key is a secret (CLAUDE.md rule 9), and generating audio for every visitor would spend credits on a public page that anyone could hammer. Generating a whole song per visitor would also give no control over tempo or key, so nothing could be changed while it plays.

## Decisions

- **Generate the sounds once, not per visitor.** `npm run jam:sounds` (`scripts/make-jam-sounds.ts`) asks ElevenLabs' sound-effects API (`/v1/sound-generation`, `eleven_text_to_sound_v2`) for 32 one-shots listed in `src/data/jam.yaml`: two drum kits, two basses, three guitar tones, piano, organ, harmonica, tenor sax, and two room loops. It runs on Joe's machine with the `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` already in his shell, shared with his other projects. The key never reaches the site, a build, or GitHub. The originals are committed in `src/assets/jam-source/` (about 1.3 MB, never served), so tuning them again costs no credits, and the build needs no key.
- **The browser is the band.** `src/lib/jam.ts` (pure, unit tested) turns a song (key, feel, tempo, form, a tone or nothing per part) into what each part plays in each bar: kick on 1 and 3, backbeat on 2 and 4, a walking or boogie bass that steps a half step into each chord change, the Jimmy Reed root-fifth-sixth comp on guitar, rootless voicings on keys, and fills from the blues scale that answer in bars 3–4, 7–8 and 11–12 (the call and response ADR 0018 was waiting on). `src/scripts/jam.ts` schedules it a bar ahead with Web Audio. Because the timing and the notes are ours, it stays on the beat and in key, and any change takes effect at the next bar.
- **Tune the notes ourselves; don't trust the prompt.** ElevenLabs mostly ignores the note a prompt asks for. It leans toward C, in whatever octave it likes, even with the frequency written into the prompt, and the harmonica came back on C5 every time. So the script measures each original's real pitch (YIN, `src/lib/audio-analysis.ts`), then renders a tuned sample every three semitones across everything the part can play, by TD-PSOLA pitch-shifting the nearest clean original (`src/lib/psola.ts`). PSOLA keeps the note's length and body, where speeding a sample up would also shorten it and thin it out. Every tuned sample is measured again and must land within 15 cents. Shifts of up to 16 semitones pass. The browser then picks the nearest tuned sample and bends it at most a semitone and a half, which a unit test checks for every note in every key and feel. That's 78 tuned samples from 18 originals, plus the drums and room loops: about 2.3 MB in all in `src/assets/jam/`. A song downloads only the samples its key and feel use.
- **Level by loudness, not by peak.** Peak normalizing left notes of one tone up to 8 dB apart (clean guitar −7 to −15 dB) and the old-record loop at −42 dB, since a spiky sound peaks long before it sounds loud. Every note and drum hit is now set to −14 dB RMS over its first 300 ms, never peaking past −1 dBFS, and the room loops to −26 dB after a soft limiter rounds off their clicks. Every tone's notes now sit within 3.5 dB of each other (a unit test checks), so the mix comes from the part levels and the patterns' accents, not from how each recording came out.
- **Played, not sequenced.** Each note moves a few milliseconds early or late and a little harder or softer (the drums stay tightest), the same way every time, so a song is repeatable. A ghost note on the snare before 4, a snare fill in triplets at the end of each 12 bars, and the guitar's turnaround in bar 11 (the root held as a pedal over a line walking down from the seventh to the fifth), then a hit on the V. The fills change from one time through the 12 to the next. Four on the hi-hat count it in.
- **The bandstand: the band, drawn, on a stage, playing along.** Under the chart, on a stage (a red valance across the top in the site's own red, and a floor drawn into each instrument that joins up across a row, with stands under the instruments that would otherwise float), each part is a line drawing in the strum neck's style: a drum kit, an upright or electric bass, a hollowbody or acoustic guitar, an upright piano or an organ, a harmonica or a tenor sax. The drawing follows the tone you pick, and a part that's off is dimmed. On each note, read off the audio clock so it's in time with what you hear, the string it's played on shakes and turns red (`src/lib/bandstand.ts` picks the string a player would use), the chord's keys go down, the drum that's hit dips, and the harmonica's hole or the sax's key lights. It's also where the band is picked: each part has a switch by its name (`role="switch"`, the same track-and-knob as the strum's sound switch), and clicking the drawing flips it too. Turning a part back on brings back the tone it had. Its tones are a segmented control under the drawing; picking one also brings a part that's off back in. Every choice on the page (key, feel, room, each part's tone) uses that one segmented control, with labels short enough to fit two instruments across a phone. Those are the form's own radios, so the page still works, and a song is still a link, without JavaScript. That's new motion, so it's recorded here. It moves only while the visitor's own Play runs, and has no CSS transitions. With reduced motion nothing shakes or dips: the colors still follow the notes. Craftpix was considered for the art and turned down: its license forbids distributing the files, and this repo is public. Its instrument art is mostly pixel-art game icons anyway.
- **The chart explains itself.** The 12 boxes have a heading and a few plain sentences under them: what a bar is, that the I chord is home, how the singer's line, its repeat and its answer fall on the three lines, and that the fills answer in the last two bars of each. Each box also shows its chord's number (I, IV or V), so the form reads the same in any key.
- **Fluid, not chopped.** Every note fades in over 4 ms (no clicks) and lets go on its own curve after its length (a time constant per part, `RELEASE` in `src/lib/jam.ts`), so it rings into the next note instead of stopping dead. The bass and the fills play legato: each note is held until the part's next one (`legato`). Drum hits play out. Stop fades the queued notes over 150 ms and lets the reverb ring out.
- **A mixing console.** Below the song settings, a channel strip per part and a master (`src/components/Mixer.astro`, audio in `src/scripts/console.ts`, numbers in `src/lib/mixer.ts`). Each strip has a fader in dB with a meter and peak hold beside it, tone (a tilt: lows and highs move opposite ways, up to 6 dB), pan, a reverb send, and mute and solo, which solo the way a desk does. The master has its level and meter, room size (the reverb's ring-out, 0.6 to 3.6 s, rebuilt when a move ends) and drive (a tanh saturation into a gentle bus compressor). The starting mix stands the band the way a small band stands: guitar a little left, keys a little right. Every move glides over about 30 ms, so riding a fader never zips or clicks. Knobs are drawn over real range inputs, and faders are horizontal ranges turned upright, so all of it works with a mouse, touch, the arrow keys and a screen reader, and draws the same in every browser. Double-click resets a control. On a phone the desk scrolls sideways inside itself; the page doesn't. The mix goes into the link as one `mix` parameter, only when it differs from the default. The meters move while the band plays and for 3 s after (the reverb tail). That's level information the visitor asked for by pressing Play, so it isn't held still under reduced motion.
- **Lead the band: choruses, not loops.** Each time around the 12 bars is a chorus of one kind (`SECTIONS` in `src/lib/jam.ts`): the tune (the fills answer the singer), a solo (the fills take all 12 bars in two-bar phrases that breathe, the last line shouting, landing on the fifth; the bass and drums dig in, the comp makes room), break it down (cross-stick and hi-hat, half-note bass, light offbeat chords, everything quieter), stop time (the band hits beat one of each bar of the first line and lays out while the soloist plays into the gaps) and the ending (the band plays the line down from the seventh together, then a stab a half step above home sliding into the last chord, with the crash). Left alone, the band arranges itself: the tune twice, then a solo, a breakdown, stop time and the tune, around (`autoSection`). The visitor can call the next chorus, call it off again, or take it home, which ends the song at bar 12 of this chorus (or the next, if it's too late) and stops on its own. The song starts on the turnaround (bars 9 to 12) as an intro, unless that's switched off. The drums crash at the top of each chorus (not when it's broken down) and now and then play a short fill at the end of a line; the bass picks a different walk now and then; the guitar climbs to the seventh every other chorus; the fills scoop into some notes from a semitone or two flat. A unit test plays every kind of chorus in every key, feel and tone and checks no note leaves its samples.
- **Sit in.** Eight pads under the stage play the blues scale (minor pentatonic plus the flat five) in the song's key, on harp, sax, guitar or piano, so there's no wrong note (`src/lib/sit-in.ts`, `src/scripts/sit-in.ts`). A pointer, a finger or the home-row keys (A to K) hold a note while pressed; dragging a held pad up bends it a whole step. The notes go straight into the instrument's channel strip, so they play whether or not the band is, show on the meters and on the stage, and the band's fills lay out for two bars after each one. Pads read "Bb" and "b3", as the key picker does (the trimmed web font has no flat sign), and screen readers hear "B flat, the flat 5".
- **Styles.** Six one-click starting points (`src/lib/styles.ts`): Chicago shuffle, Texas slow blues, Delta porch, Jump boogie, Late-night slow burn and Motor City. Each is a whole song and mix, so picking one is the same as following its link, and it stays marked until something changes. They're kinds of blues, not claims about anyone.
- **Record a take.** The console's master output goes to a MediaRecorder on request, and the take comes back as a download link named after the song (`blues-in-e-shuffle-100-bpm.webm`, or Ogg or MP4, whatever the browser records). Nothing is uploaded.
- **A song is a link.** The controls are a plain GET form, mirrored into the address bar, so a song can be shared.
- **Honest about what it is.** The page says the sounds were generated with ElevenLabs and aren't Johnny or his band, and it points to the shows. It makes no claims about Johnny, so it needs nothing from the facts ledger.

## Why it passes the "worth it" test

It doesn't build itself from Johnny's data, so it has to serve an audience the home page can't. It does: people who play with the strum and want more, and anyone who wants to hear how a 12-bar fits together, without bloating the home page. The home page keeps its budget. Its only change is a "Play it with a band" link under the strum. The page's script and sounds load only on `/jam/`, and only the sounds the song uses (29 files, about 560 KB, for the default band with every kind of chorus ready; the crash cymbals are the biggest).

## Consequences

- The bandstand puts a red "Solo" tag on whoever has the spotlight: the fills in a solo or stop-time chorus, or the instrument you're sitting in on.
- One more page in the sitemap. It isn't in `llms.txt`, which is about Johnny.
- No new third parties at runtime and no CSP change: the sounds are same-origin, hashed under `/_astro/`.
- New typeface or color: none. New motion: the bandstand (above), still under reduced motion, and only while the visitor's own Play runs. The playing bar is a red fill, a state change, not an animation.
- Regenerating a sound costs ElevenLabs credits and changes the measured pitch, so rerun the unit tests after `npm run jam:sounds`.
